CCOMPREHEND

What situation are we actually in?

A mature title with a working game and a leaking door

Nine and a half years old, 50M+ installs, 4.36★, still shipping monthly. This is not a dying game — it's a game that stopped converting the people it still reaches.

A mature live game has exactly two honest options: extract harder from the players who remain, or widen the base again. Which one you pick determines every downstream decision, so it's worth settling first.

The store data settles it. WWE Champions carries 50M+ installs and a 4.36★ rating across 402,333 ratings — 70% of them five-star. Whales on a title this age are already monetised close to their ceiling, so extraction has little headroom and a real goodwill cost. Widening the base is the brief.

And the licence isn't the problem, which is the most useful thing the competitive set tells us. WWE SuperCard is rated 4.77 by 2.6× as many iOS raters — wrestling fans will happily five-star a WWE mobile game; they're doing it next door. Empires & Puzzles launched five weeks later, same genre, no licence at all, and carries 3.4× the iOS ratings at 4.69. Same nine years, same mechanic, no IP advantage. Whatever is costing this title goodwill, it isn't the audience and it isn't the wrestling.

The consequence for reach

If you can't out-bid the casual-puzzle giants for installs — and at this age and budget you can't — then you have to out-convert them. That puts every point of first-session retention to work twice: once as retention, and again as a cut to what an install effectively costs. Reach stops being a spending problem and becomes a conversion problem.

RREPORT

What do these players actually need?

They don't need a better game. They need to survive long enough to find it.

Across 2,945 reviews, affection for the game survives the one-star rating intact. What replaces it is a vocabulary of money — and, underneath that, a support problem nobody's counting.

The word “fun” appears at the same rate in 1–2★ reviews as in 5★ reviews — a skew of −1.1 points. “Money” skews +26.7, and is the most-used word in the whole corpus.

n = 2,945 · iOS + Android · the finding everything else rests on

That is not what a dying game looks like. A dying game loses the loop — players stop finding it fun and say so. Here the loop is intact and something else is driving people out. That's a fixable problem wrapped around a working product.

Four of the six problem themes are retention problems, not revenue problems. That is the whole argument in one table: the loudest complaint is about money, but the majority of distinct complaint types are about people being unable to keep playing.

The theme that was missing

Bugs & support reaches 20% of reviews at 2.42★ — the second-largest theme by reach, and 588 reviews deep. It didn't appear anywhere in the original write-up, and it corroborates independently: three of the ten most negative-skewing words in the entire corpus are “support” (+9.6), “scopely” (+8.5) and “customer” (+8.3), and “fix” is used 252 times, putting it in the top fifteen words overall.

Four separate signals pointing at the same thing is not noise. It doesn't change the recommendation — the on-ramp is still the bigger prize — but it belongs in the week-one data pull, because a support backlog is usually cheaper to fix than a retention feature is to build.

The limit worth stating before anyone finds it

Written reviews skew negative by construction: only 8.1% of raters write text, and angry players write. And onboarding complaints are just 3% of the corpus — which is exactly what you'd expect, because players who churn on day one never come back to write a review.

So the segment this whole case is about is the one segment that is structurally silent in the evidence. That absence is the argument for pulling the funnel data, not a hole in the argument.

CCUT

How do we choose between the options?

Late-curve retention compounds. Early-curve retention doesn't.

Three filters, applied in order: does it move the constrained KPI, does it compound, and does it risk the golden goose?

The first filter is the ledger at the top of this page. Reach is not the constraint and revenue is not to be touched, so anything that doesn't move retention has to justify itself unusually hard.

The second filter is where the interesting result is. Fit a power-law retention curve through D1 and D7 and integrate it over 180 days, and a D7 improvement is worth roughly 5× a D1 improvement of comparable effort — because moving the late curve lifts the whole tail, while moving D1 lifts one day. That single fact reorders the portfolio and is the reason the highest-value bet is not the most obvious one.

The third filter is risk. This game's revenue works. Any bet that touches drop rates, pack pricing or the gacha core can damage a functioning economy faster than a retention gain can repay it — so those bets stay gated behind data rather than shipped on judgement.

What we are explicitly not doing

Not extracting harder. No new pack tiers, no deeper subscription ladder. The corpus shows what that costs in goodwill and the whale ceiling is close anyway.

Not rebuilding the economy. It works. The two lead bets touch no economy surface at all — they raise the free-to-play floor without lowering the whale ceiling, which is possible because whales pay for relative advantage and status, not absolute power.

Not buying our way out. Out-bidding the casual-puzzle giants for installs is the one move this title cannot afford.

LLIST

What could we build?

Five bets, weighted toward the constrained KPI

Three move retention, one protects revenue, one recovers reach. The weighting is the recommendation.

1
Retention · D1 + D7

“The Debut” — a guided first session

Defer every economy surface until the player has a roster and a win. Session one becomes a guaranteed marquee pull, one clear win, and one next verb. Systems reveal on a schedule instead of arriving all at once, and the store stays shut until there's a team to spend on.

Mechanism. Cuts time-to-first-reward and removes every session-one dead-end, so more installs survive to the point where the loop can hook them.

$225k6–8 wks · 1 pod
2
Retention · D7 + D30

“Rookie Crew” — a social on-ramp before League 5

Auto-draft every new player into a crew of four to eight within minutes of install, with one opt-in veteran mentor and a shared low-stakes goal. Curated chat replaces the global recruitment dump until League 4. At League 5 the crew graduates into a real faction together.

Mechanism. Faction membership is likely the strongest retention correlate in the game, and it is currently locked for the entire window in which players decide whether to stay.

$1.12M8–10 wks · 1 pod + mod
3
Retention · D30

Mid-game catch-up track

An event-gated coin faucet aimed at the specific band where progression stalls — not a global giveaway. Targeted at the cohort the retention curve identifies, so it recovers churn without discounting the players who were going to pay through the wall anyway.

Mechanism. Inverts the faucet-to-sink ratio for non-payers at the stall point, turning a motivation cliff into a slope.

$696k4–6 wks · live-ops
4
Revenue · trust

Dupe protection and published odds

Duplicates convert to shards toward a star the player chooses, and pull probabilities are published. Ship to a test cohort first and read the trust and retention effect before going wide.

Mechanism. Trust is the lowest-sentiment theme in the corpus at 2.06★. Published odds are cheap, already regulatory table-stakes in several markets, and answer the loudest constructive complaint players make.

$574k5–7 wks · economy
5
Reach · reactivation

Tentpole win-back engine

Personalised reactivation tied to a lapsed player's own top superstar and timed to WrestleMania and Royal Rumble. The cheapest re-acquisition available on a title with 50M lifetime installs.

Mechanism. A dormant pool this large, against a live WWE calendar of natural return moments, is currently addressed with generic push if at all.

$376k3–4 wks · CRM

Base-case annualised incremental revenue at the model's assumptions. Two of those inputs — monthly installs and ARPDAU — are unverified estimates, so treat the ranking as the output and the absolute figures as illustrative.

Read this before you read the numbers

The result is highly sensitive to the decay exponent: a ~3-point D7 lift changes lifetime user-days by roughly a third under a power-law fit. That is why bet 2 outranks bet 1 despite bet 1 moving the more visible metric.

That sensitivity isn't a flaw to hide — it is the argument for measuring rather than modelling. If your real curve is flatter or steeper than the genre benchmark, the ranking can move. The scale-invariant part of this model is the only part I'd defend without your telemetry.

So why does F1 ship first if F2 is worth five times more? Three reasons, and they're all sequencing rather than value. F1 is cheaper and faster. F1 clears the session-one dead-ends that would otherwise cap F2's ceiling. And most importantly, shipping F2 first would draft new players into crews they'd have quit before ever reaching — you'd be measuring a social feature against a cohort the on-ramp had already lost.

SSUMMARISE

So what should we do on Monday?

Six data pulls, then two builds with pre-agreed kill criteria

Nothing here needs to be taken on faith. Week one replaces my assumptions with your telemetry, and either confirms the ranking or kills it before a line of code is written.

  1. Pull the cohort retention table (D1/D7/D30) by lifecycle stage × spend tier. This alone confirms or kills the ranking.
  2. Query the new-user funnel step by step: install → tutorial → first pull → first faction join → first event. Find the largest single drop.
  3. Correlate “joined an active faction” against D7 and D30. If faction-join isn't a top-2 retention correlate, bet 2 gets re-scoped before it's built.
  4. Map faucet vs. sink per cohort to locate the stall band precisely, rather than assuming where the wall is.
  5. Pull historical dupe rate and free-reward tier distribution to confirm or refute the eroding-value hypothesis behind bet 4.
  6. Pull support ticket volume and resolution time against churn — the theme the review corpus says is the second-largest and that nobody has sized.

Then build, in this order:

What I'd want to be wrong about

That faction-join is the top retention correlate. It's the load-bearing assumption under the highest-value bet, and I've inferred it rather than measured it. Pull 3 tests it before anything gets built.

That the late-2025 team-mode change hurt. Two heavily-upvoted reviews say team modes were stripped back and players left. Two reviews are a signal, not a finding — and if that change was deliberate, bet 2 runs against a recent strategic decision and that conversation happens before the spec is approved, not after.

That the benchmark retention curve resembles yours. If your curve is materially flatter, bets 3 and 4 move up the list.
