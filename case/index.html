The metric, taken apart

Where WWE Champions stands on each factor

Payers ÷ DAU

Never been the target.

Little product work has gone into the first purchase. The League 2 shelf's cheapest item is a $14.99 starter pack on a six-hour timer, under a $99.99 headline pack — and the first thing the game asks you to buy is a rescue from a defeat.

The lever

ARPPU

At its ceiling, and defended.

DAU

The denominator. Growing it can hurt.

Retention work adds users who are overwhelmingly non-payers. That grows revenue and flattens or dilutes the metric we're judged on — which is why this case reorders the previous one rather than extending it.

Not the lever

A correction · 8 August

An earlier version of this case said the League 2 featured shelf held exactly one offer, at $99.99, and that the entry price band was empty. Re-reading my own source screenshot to build the prototype, that is wrong. Beneath the $99.99 League Essentials pack, the same screen carries a 2-Star Gold Andre Starter Pack at $14.99, marked “One Time Offer”, on a 05h 42m countdown.

So the floor is $14.99, not $99.99. What changed: move 02 now adds two rungs beneath an existing pack rather than building a ladder from nothing, its modelled lift drops from +0.55pp to +0.35pp, and the programme's base case falls from $418k to $367k a year and +2.2% ARPDAU. The conclusion holds; the size of one move does not.

What did not change, and is arguably worse than the original claim: there is still nothing between free and $14.99 — three to five times the conventional entry price for this genre — and the cheapest way into the game is a countdown timer pointed at somebody who installed this afternoon. That is the finding, and it is better sourced than the one it replaces.

CCOMPREHEND

What situation are we actually in?

One factor is maxed. The other has never been built.

A mature title with a working loop, a defended whale ceiling, and an entry price band whose floor is $14.99. ARPDAU is decided in a player's first week — and we price that week for somebody who has already decided to spend.

A mature live game has two honest ways to raise revenue per user: take more from the people who already pay, or turn more of the people who don't into people who do. Which one you pick determines every downstream decision, so it's worth settling first — and it settles quickly.

Extraction is out. WWE Champions carries 50M+ installs and a 4.36★ rating across 402,333 ratings. Whales on a title this age are already monetised close to their ceiling, so there is little headroom — and the review corpus prices the goodwill cost precisely: “money” is the most-used word across 2,945 reviews, and it skews +26.7 points toward one star. That leaves conversion.

And conversion is a first-week problem. In mid-core RPGs the large majority of players who ever pay make their first purchase within days of installing; the payer / non-payer split is set early and rarely re-opens. So ARPDAU on this title is decided in the same ten minutes the previous version of this case worried about — but the thing to fix in those ten minutes isn't retention. It's the shelf, and specifically the bottom of it.

The licence isn't the problem either, which is the most useful thing the competitive set tells us. WWE SuperCard is rated 4.77 by 2.6× as many iOS raters, and Empires & Puzzles launched five weeks later, same genre, no licence at all, and carries 3.4× the iOS ratings at 4.69. Same nine years, same mechanic, no IP advantage. Whatever is costing this title goodwill, it isn't the audience and it isn't the wrestling.

The consequence

If the whale ceiling is fixed and the payer base is the variable, then a point of first-purchase conversion is worth more than a point of retention — because it moves the numerator directly instead of adding to the denominator and hoping the mix holds. Growing revenue and growing ARPDAU are not the same job, and this title has only ever been given the first one.

RREPORT

What is actually stopping them?

The loop is loved. The transaction is hated.

Across 2,945 reviews, affection for the game survives the one-star rating intact. What replaces it is a vocabulary of money — which is not the sound of people who don't want to spend.

The word “fun” appears at the same rate in 1–2★ reviews as in 5★ reviews — a skew of −1.1 points. “Money” skews +26.7, and is the most-used word in the whole corpus.

n = 2,945 · iOS + Android · the finding everything else rests on

That is not the sound of a dying game, and it is not the sound of an audience that resents paying. People who don't care about spending don't talk about money. This is high intent meeting a hostile transaction — a conversion problem, and conversion problems are cheaper to fix than desire problems.

The two shaded rows are the first purchase — and they are the loudest and the angriest themes in the corpus. Money is the most-reached complaint at 36%. Trust is the lowest-rated at 2.06★. Both sit directly on P0→P1, and neither has ever been the subject of product work.

The theme that reframes the second purchase

Bugs & support reaches 20% of reviews at 2.42★ — the second-largest theme by reach, 588 reviews deep — and it didn't appear anywhere in the original write-up. It corroborates independently: three of the ten most negative-skewing words in the corpus are “support” (+9.6), “scopely” (+8.5) and “customer” (+8.3), and “fix” appears 252 times.

Under a retention thesis this was a side-finding. Under this one it's structural: a support failure is a second-purchase killer. What decides whether a first-time payer ever pays again is what happens when something goes wrong with the thing they bought. It stays a week-one data pull, and it is usually cheaper to fix than a feature is to build.

The limit worth stating before anyone finds it

Written reviews skew negative by construction: only 8.1% of raters write text, and angry players write. Onboarding complaints are just 3% of the corpus — exactly what you'd expect, because players who churn on day one never come back to write a review.

This cohort is doubly silent: the day-one churner doesn't write, and neither does the player who looked at a $99.99 shelf and quietly closed it. Nobody writes a review about a purchase they didn't make. That absence is the argument for pulling the funnel data, not a hole in the argument — and it's why the first thing this case asks for is a query, not a budget.

CCUT

How do we choose between the options?

Three filters, and one asymmetry that does most of the work

Does it move the numerator? Does it compound? Does it risk the golden goose? Applied in that order, five bets become one programme.

Filter one — numerator, not denominator. The decomposition at the top of this page does most of the cutting on its own. ARPPU is defended and DAU is the denominator, so anything that doesn't raise the share of players who pay has to justify itself unusually hard.

Filter two — does it compound? A single well-placed offer converts someone once. A ladder converts them and then gives them somewhere to go. Repeat-purchase rate is the compounding term in this model, which is why the recommendation is a three-rung structure rather than one starter pack — and why the second and third purchase get as much design attention as the first.

Filter three — risk to the golden goose. This game's revenue works. Anything that touches whale pricing or existing gacha drop rates can damage a functioning economy faster than a conversion gain repays it. Deterministic starter bundles are the safe surface: they add rungs below the existing floor without moving any rung already on it.

Converting a non-payer buys you retention.
Retaining a non-payer does not buy you a payer.

The asymmetry that orders the whole portfolio

That asymmetry is why first-purchase conversion outranks the retention bets even on retention grounds, and it's where the downstream D7 and D30 effect comes from: payers retain far better than non-payers, so a conversion win is a retention win you get for free — while a retention win is not a conversion win at all. One caveat, stated plainly: that relationship is correlational until it's matched on session count, because payers were always the more engaged players. That match is data pull 3, and the downstream line is deliberately the smallest of the three revenue components in the model, so the case doesn't lean on it.

What we are explicitly not doing

Not extracting harder. No new top-end pack tiers, no deeper subscription ladder. This is a case for more payers, not bigger ones — the two get confused constantly and they have opposite effects on the corpus.

Not touching existing drop rates. Published odds describe the current rates; they don't change them. The starter rungs are deterministic, so the entry band contains no gacha at all.

Not discounting the whale ladder. Every rung is once-per-account, time-boxed, and shares no contents with a whale SKU. Top-decile ARPPU is a kill metric, monitored from day one of the test rather than checked afterwards.

LLIST

What do we build?

One programme: “The First Dollar”

Three moves on one causal chain. Each unblocks the next, and none of them works alone — which is what makes this a programme and not a menu.

01
Numerator · P0→P1

Earn the ask

No monetisation surface before a win. The first match cannot be lost, so the first thing the game ever asks you to buy stops being a rescue from a defeat. The store opens after a victory, not before a roster.

Mechanism. Today the first offer is a loss-tax shown to someone with three superstars and no win. Moving the ask behind the first win changes what's being sold from relief to momentum — the only framing an entry purchase converts on.

$129k+0.25pp conv6–8 wks · 1 pod
02
Numerator · P0→P1→P2

Build the rung

Two rungs below the existing $14.99 starter pack, which becomes the top of the entry ladder rather than its floor. Both deterministic — the player sees exactly what they get — and built around a superstar they've already used. Rung 1 appears at the first wall, rung 2 within 72 hours of rung 1. Once per account, and the six-hour countdown goes.

Mechanism. The chain depends on this move and it is still the smallest of the three by value — because a $14.99 rung already exists, so this deepens a ladder rather than creating one. It is priced honestly below, not talked up.

$89k+0.35pp conv5–7 wks · 1 pod + economy
03
Numerator · P1→P2→P3

Make it safe to buy

Publish the pull probabilities. Convert duplicates to shards toward a star the player chooses. Scoped to the entry band first, where the rungs are deterministic anyway — so it touches no existing drop rate.

Mechanism. Trust is the lowest-sentiment theme in the corpus at 2.06★, and a first purchase is an act of trust. This is also the only move that lifts repeat purchase, which is where the model's compounding lives.

$148k+0.20pp conv4–6 wks · economy

Base-case annualised incremental revenue at the model's assumptions, which are stated and editable on the evidence page. Move 03 scores highest despite being the cheapest because it's the only one that lifts repeat rate — and the repeat assumption is the softest input in the model, which is exactly why data pull 4 exists. Move 02, the one the other two depend on, is now the smallest of the three by value: a $14.99 pack already exists, so it deepens a ladder instead of creating one. Structurally load-bearing and financially third — both are true, and the sequencing argument below is what justifies building it anyway.

Why in this order — each necessary, none sufficient

02 without 01

A great offer in front of someone who hasn't won yet. The rung exists; nobody has been given a reason to want it.

02 without 03

They convert once and never twice. The entry purchase lands, the second one is a black box, and the ladder stops at rung one.

01 + 03 without 02

A clean, honest, well-sequenced shop whose cheapest item is still $14.99. Better, and still priced past the player we are trying to convert.

Parked, and the condition each one is waiting on

Rookie Crew
The social on-ramp before League 5. A D30 play, and a good one — but it retains players rather than converting them, so it builds the denominator. Ships once there's a payer base worth retaining, and remains gated on whether faction-join is a top-2 retention correlate.
Mid-game catch-up track
Blocked on faucet-vs-sink data, as before. Also a denominator play: it keeps people alive rather than converting them, so it moves behind the programme on this metric.
Tentpole win-back
Cheap, and the dormant pool is enormous. Sequenced after move 02, so returning players land on a real ladder instead of the shelf that lost them the first time.
Deeper whale extraction
Rejected outright. No headroom, and the corpus prices the goodwill cost. This programme adds payers; it does not enlarge the ones we have.

EEVALUATE

What are we trading off?

This programme is worth less money — and it's still the right call

The retention portfolio produces the bigger revenue number. It also cannot raise ARPDAU, because it grows the denominator it's measured against. That's the whole trade, put as bluntly as I can put it.

Both rows scored the same way against the same baseline: total DAU 121,212, ARPDAU $0.2200. Marginal retained users are assumed to monetise at half the average — see below for why, and for what happens if you don't assume it.

The correction behind that table, and the one assumption it turns on

Scored the previous model's own way — every added user monetising at the full average ARPDAU — the retention leads are worth $1,344,463 a year and move ARPDAU by +0.2%. Neutral. And neutral by construction: that model multiplies added user-days by average ARPDAU, so the ratio cannot move. The answer was fixed before the model ran.

The correction is a single assumption: the users a retention bet adds are marginal survivors of the activation window, and they convert to payer well below the average rate. Score them at half and the same bets read −6.0%. Score them at the full average and they read flat. There is no assumption under which a retention bet raises ARPDAU — that isn't a modelling choice, it's what arithmetic does when you add to a denominator.

The First Dollar is robust to the same correction — $366,673 corrected against $431,803 uncorrected — because its gain comes from the numerator rather than from added user-days. That robustness, not the headline dollar figure, is the argument.

So take the smaller number seriously rather than hiding it. $672k of retention revenue is real money and I'd take it. But if ARPDAU is the number on the scorecard, a bigger revenue figure earned by adding non-payers is the wrong trade — it looks like progress in the board pack and reads as decline in the metric. The two jobs are different, and this case is scoped to the one that was asked for.

Where this model is softest, in order

1 · The marginal converter's value — $21 over 180 days. Adding a cheap rung raises conversion and lowers average first-purchase value. The model deliberately prices an added payer at a third of an existing converter's $62 for that reason — but the gap between those two figures is modelled, not measured, and it drives the headline more than anything else here.

2 · The repeat-rate lift. It's the compounding term, and it's carrying move 03 to the top of the table on the softest evidence in the document.

3 · The baseline itself. Ever-pay at 2.0% and daily payer rate at 3.0% are genre benchmarks, not your numbers. Every one of these is a week-one query, not a research project.

SSUMMARISE

So what should we do on Monday?

Four queries, then one programme with its kill criterion written first

Nothing here needs to be taken on faith. Week one replaces my assumptions with your telemetry, and pulls 1 and 2 together either confirm this programme or kill it before a line of code is written.

  1. The payer funnel by session index. Install → session 1…7 → first store impression → first purchase. Where does a first purchase happen today, how long does it take, and at what price? This is the query the whole case is asking for.
  2. Price-point distribution of first purchases, and the SKU ladder a new account can actually see. Is there anything under $10 in front of a new player? What's the modal first-purchase price? This confirms or kills move 02.
  3. D7 and D30 for payers vs non-payers, matched on session count. The matching is the point — unmatched, it only tells you that engaged players spend. Sizes the downstream retention line and tests the asymmetry the whole priority order rests on.
  4. Second-purchase rate and time-to-second, for first-time payers. Sizes the compounding half of the model, currently its softest input.

The kill criterion, written before the work starts

If pull 2 shows a sub-$10 SKU already visible to new players, and pull 1 shows ever-pay conversion at or above the 2% benchmark, move 02 is wrong. The floor isn't $14.99, the premise fails, and this collapses back to a retention case — in which event the parked list is the real portfolio, and I'd say so.

I'd rather have that written down now than discover it in week three. And I'd rather say plainly that $14.99 is not sub-$10, so this criterion technically survived the correction above — I don't want the threshold doing work the evidence should be doing. Pull 2 answers it properly.

What I'd want to be wrong about

That nothing sits below $14.99. I have already been wrong once here — I read the same shelf as holding a single $99.99 offer and missed the starter pack underneath it. That is corrected above, and it is the reason to trust the pull rather than the screenshot: this case rests on one shelf, one account, League 2, one day, and storefronts are routinely geo- and cohort-tested. Pull 2, ahead of everything else.

That payer retention is causal rather than selection. Payers retain better; payers were also always the more engaged players. Pull 3 matches on session count to separate the two, and the downstream retention line is deliberately the smallest of the three revenue components so the case doesn't lean on it.

That a cheap rung doesn't cannibalise the whale ladder. The guardrails — once per account, time-boxed, no shared contents — are designed for exactly this, but designs fail. Top-decile ARPPU is a kill metric on move 02 from day one of the test, not a post-hoc check.
